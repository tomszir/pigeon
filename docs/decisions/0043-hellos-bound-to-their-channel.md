# 0043: Hellos bound to their channel

A `hello` in a pair or self room signs a nonce the receiving connection sent and both DTLS certificate fingerprints of the data channel carrying it, so a hello forwarded or replayed from another connection proves nothing.

- Status: accepted
- Date: 2026-11-26

## Context

An envelope is signed over the chat's id, its time and its body, and nothing in it names the connection carrying it. A pair session took the first signed `hello` on a connection as proof that the connection was that device. Anyone who could sit in a pair or self room could therefore forward a friend's hello from one connection to another and be taken for the friend: a removed device still holding the pair and self secrets, or anyone who knows both ids while two friends are still on the old `pair:` room. The friend's Pigeon then sent that connection new messages, and a replayed `log-sync` got the chat's history. The same replays brought back an old `unfriend`, `buzz` or `hello` (an old name, old Lighthouses), and an old hello of your own raised a false "open in another browser" warning. An audit reproduced it in core with the in-memory network.

Constraints: use a standard construction rather than a freshness scheme of our own; long-lived keys stay non-extractable in WebCrypto; friends on older Pigeons should keep working where that's safe.

Each WebRTC data channel already runs over DTLS. Each side's SDP names its certificate in `a=fingerprint`, and the browser refuses a DTLS handshake whose certificate doesn't match the fingerprint it was given. So the two certificates DTLS used identify one channel, and whoever writes into it holds the matching certificate key. The SDP alone isn't proof of that after the handshake: a peer can send a new description later (Trystero renegotiates over the data channel), and Firefox shows it as the current remote description while the old channel keeps running for some seconds. Signing them is channel binding (RFC 5056), the standard way to tie an authentication done above a secure channel to that channel; RFC 8844 describes the same relay against identities carried over DTLS in WebRTC.

## Decision

- **`Room.binding(peer)`** gives a connection's `ChannelBinding`, `{ local, remote }`, SHA-256 fingerprints as lowercase hex with no colons. `local` is from this browser's own description, which only it writes. `remote` is the SHA-256 of the certificate the data channel's DTLS transport checked (`sctp.transport.getRemoteCertificates()`), and the remote description must name that same certificate. It's undefined when the channel isn't connected, the browser can't say which certificate DTLS checked, a description names anything but exactly one certificate in that form (any other `a=fingerprint` line, another hash among them, or two different ones), or the remote description names another certificate than DTLS checked; or when there's no direct peer connection (Trystero can carry a peer through other peers; such a peer has no channel of its own to bind to). A connection that can't be bound isn't talked to: failing closed rather than falling back to the description is the point. Chromium and Firefox give both and agree; a browser that can't say which certificate DTLS checked would meet friends only through Lighthouses. Sends to one peer arrive in the order they were sent, whatever their size (Trystero sends a large message in chunks, which a small one sent after it could otherwise overtake), so a device log always arrives ahead of the nonce after it. The browser transport reads the binding from Trystero's peer connections, and the in-memory test network gives each connection a certificate of its own.
- **A nonce first.** On every new connection in a pair or self room, after its device logs, each side sends `nonce`: 16 random bytes in base32, in an envelope like any other. A side answers only a nonce signed by a key it takes there (one of the friend's devices, one of your own, or this device's own key, for a copied profile). It answers each nonce once, at most 8 on a connection, because a peer that reconnects keeps its Trystero id and greets again; a replayed nonce only gets a hello bound to the replayer's own channel, and replaying the same few can't make it sign more.
- **A bound hello.** The answer is `hello` with `binding: { nonce, local, remote }`: the nonce it answers and the fingerprints as the sender sees them, its own first. The receiver takes it only when the nonce is the one it sent on that connection and `local` and `remote` are its own view swapped, comparing decoded bytes. Each part has one accepted spelling. A hello forwarded from another connection names that other channel, and a relay that passes nonces through to get a fresh hello still has a certificate of its own on each side, so it fails. Fingerprints alone would stop the relay; the nonce also makes each hello this connection's, even between two browsers that kept the same certificates. A hello on `announce` (a rename) signs the same connection's nonce again.
- **Nothing else counts until then.** Every other envelope counts only on a connection proven by a bound hello, and only from the device whose hello proved it; another device's hello can't take a proven connection over. Presence, profile and `log-sync` go to a connection only once it has proved itself. When a connection leaves, it's forgotten at once, not behind the messages still being read from it, so nothing more is sent to an id someone else may take next (Trystero hands a gone peer's id to whoever connects under it), including replies already queued for it and still being signed, and a hello from before signs a nonce that's gone. Each envelope is read against the connection as it was when the envelope arrived, with its nonce and channel taken then: one received before the leave from the device that proved it still counts (last messages, an `unfriend` sent just before leaving, as a farewell does), and so does a hello received before the leave but read after it, which proves what came after it without showing anyone online; what would answer the connection or change who it is (`bye`, `log-sync`) and typing don't, and nothing received under the id afterwards counts as that device. What's kept for a connection goes once it has left and everything it sent has been read. Whether the device is still taken is asked again after the channel is read, so a removal taken in meanwhile counts. DTLS keeps anyone else from writing into that channel, so a replayed `unfriend`, `buzz`, `log-sync` or `hello` has no connection on which it would count. Taking `buzz`, `unfriend` and the secret envelopes once stays, for a friend in two rooms at once. The copied-profile warning comes only from a bound hello of this device's own key.
- **Older Pigeons.** A Pigeon from before this sends hellos without `binding` and drops `nonce` as an unknown type, so it never answers one. Between it and an updated Pigeon nothing on the live connection counts either way: each shows the other offline, with no presence, typing, buzz, live chat or live catch-up between them. What can still pass, because it's signed on its own and doesn't depend on the connection, does: device logs sent on joining, and everything through Lighthouses, so chat messages go as letters while none of the friend's devices is connected, a removal reaches them as a farewell in their mailbox, and your own devices keep the device log, profile and Lighthouse list in step through the self mailbox. Friends from before pair secrets can't move out of the old pair room until both have updated, since that move runs on a live session. A friend with one device updated and another not gets no letters while the updated one is online, so the other catches up once it updates; nothing is lost, only late. The web app updates on a reload, so this lasts only until the friend's next one.
- **What an un-updated Pigeon still risks.** It still takes an unbound hello, so on its side the old problem stands: someone in the room could still forward a hello to it and be taken for its friend, and an updated Pigeon can't fix a Pigeon that doesn't check. Updating closes it.

### For later

- **Replacing the pair and self secrets when a device is removed.** A removed device keeps the secrets it was given and can still enter those rooms. With binding it can't pass for anyone (and its own key is refused once it's off the list), but it can still see when the others are in the rooms. Replacing both secrets on removal would shut it out; that needs its own change and record.
- **Pairing and linking** run in their own rooms with their own messages; binding them the same way is their own change.

## Consequences

A connection in a pair or self room counts as a device only once that device has signed this channel, so a relay or a replay can't stand in for a friend or one of your own devices, and nothing new had to be invented: the binding is to the DTLS channel WebRTC already authenticates. Saying hello takes one more message each way. A transport now has to say which channel each connection is; one that can't (a peer carried through other peers) can't be talked to in pair and self rooms. Until a friend updates, the two of you meet only through Lighthouses. A Noise handshake over the data channel was the heavier alternative: it would need X25519 static keys bound to each device and a channel layer of our own, where binding to DTLS needs neither.
