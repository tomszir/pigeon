# Threat model

Whom Pigeon defends against, what each of them can learn or do, what stops them, and what Pigeon doesn't try to defend against, with a table of every protocol message and the checks made before it counts.

This page describes what the code in `@pigeon/core` and the Messenger does. [protocol.md](protocol.md) says how each part works, [flows.md](flows.md) draws the handshakes, and [state-machines.md](state-machines.md) draws pairing, linking and recovery as states.

## What Pigeon protects

- **What you say.** Chat entries go only over a WebRTC data channel, encrypted end to end by DTLS, to your friend's devices and your own, or as mail sealed to those devices' keys. Nobody else can read them.
- **That what you say is yours.** Everything you send is signed by a device on your device log. Nobody can write as you, or change or move what you wrote.
- **Who you talk to.** Pair rooms, your devices' self room and your mail tags are named from secrets only the two ends hold, and change every UTC day, so knowing your id finds none of them.
- **Who you are.** Which devices speak for you changes only by the device that writes your device log, or by your recovery key.
- **Where you are, as far as you choose.** A friend's Pigeon learns your IP address unless you turn on Always Go Through the Relay; a Lighthouse you didn't choose is never reached at a private address, and never while that's on.

## Scope

Pigeon is a messenger for friends and small groups, and what it defends against is scoped to match, in two tiers.

**Defended in full**, designed against, modelled and reviewed:

- Confidentiality of messages and of every key and secret, including against removed devices once their removal is known.
- Forgery and impersonation: no one can speak as you or as a friend.
- Anything that breaks normal use with no attacker involved: lost or stalled mail, sync that stops, crashes.

**Accepted**, with the residual stated under [Accepted residuals](#accepted-residuals) and no mechanism added for it: resource exhaustion by an attacker with many networks, metadata others on your network can infer from limits and wakes, consistency games by one of your own devices before it is gone from your list, and a hostile Lighthouse making a client fetch a lot.

An attack that reads a message, a key or a secret, or lets someone speak as you or a friend, belongs to the first tier whatever it costs the attacker.

## Who it defends against

Each heading is the name the message table uses for that attacker.

### Relays

The public Nostr relays that carry signaling (the offers that set a connection up), the TURN relay that carries a data channel when two peers can't reach each other directly, and anyone else on the network path between two Pigeons.

They can learn:

- Which room topics are in use, when each peer joins and leaves, and the IP address each connects from. A live code's nameplate is public, so they can tell when a code's room is in use. Pair and self room names come from a secret and change every UTC day, so a relay can't tell whose room it is from their ids, but it can see the same address join the same rooms day after day.
- The offers in invite and device-link rooms, which have no password, and so the addresses in them. Offers in pair and self rooms are encrypted with a password made from the room's secret.
- On the TURN relay: both ends' addresses, and when and how much they send, never what. The data channel's DTLS runs end to end through it.

They can do:

- Drop, delay or replay anything, and keep two peers from meeting. Pigeon can't prevent that; mail through Lighthouses is the way round it.
- Sit in the middle of a connection, with a certificate of their own on each side. In pair and self rooms a `hello` signs the nonce sent on its connection and both certificate fingerprints, so one they forward from their other side names the wrong channel and doesn't count, and nothing else counts on a connection until a `hello` does. A live code's handshake puts both fingerprints in the transcript both confirmations cover, so neither confirmation holds through them.
- Join a live code's room by its nameplate and guess its password. Nothing they see lets them test a guess offline, since the password only goes into SPAKE2: each guess is a wrong try the code's maker counts and shows, and five stop the code, so a guess wins with odds of 5 in 2^30. Stopping the code that way is something they can do.

### Lighthouses

The always-on peers that hold sealed mail, yours and any a friend's Pigeon leaves at theirs. [lighthouse.md](lighthouse.md#what-a-lighthouse-stores-sees-and-can-do) says in full what one stores, sees and can do.

- They can't open mail: it's sealed to each recipient device's key. They can't tell whose it is: a tag is a keyed hash of a secret only the two ends hold, the recipient and the day. An invite link's card is sealed under the link's secret, which a Lighthouse never sees.
- They see the IP address of whoever puts and fetches, when, how much, and under which tags, so they can tell that whoever put mail under a tag and whoever fetched it are talking, and that the same address comes back.
- They can withhold or lose mail. Senders put it at every Lighthouse on the recipient's list and again every 3 days until each device's receipt comes.
- They can make a client spend work: a Lighthouse can list as many blobs under your tags as it likes, and your client gets and tries each one meant for you or your friends, at most 64 KB and 64 wraps a blob, each wrap one ECDH and a few HKDFs ([lighthouse.md](lighthouse.md#what-a-lighthouse-stores-sees-and-can-do)). Until it's taken off your list, a hostile one can keep your client busy for as long as it answers. Pigeon doesn't limit this; use Lighthouses you trust ([Accepted residuals](#accepted-residuals)).
- They can't pass off a reply as another Lighthouse's, or replay one: each reply is signed with the Lighthouse's key over the client's nonce for the connection and the hash of the exact request.
- A Lighthouse you didn't choose (named in a friend's `hello`, an invite link or a card) would learn your IP address if you connected, so Pigeon reaches one only at a `wss://` address on a public host, and never while Always Go Through the Relay is on ([protocol.md](protocol.md#lighthouses-you-didnt-choose)).

### Strangers

Anyone else on the internet.

- They don't hold your pair or self secrets, so they can't find those rooms or your mail tags, or enter the rooms, which need the password made from the secret.
- In a room they can name (a code's nameplate) a connection counts for nothing: a code's handshake needs the password, and in pair and self rooms nothing counts until a `hello` signed by a device the other side already accepts.
- They can guess at a live code as relays can, with the same odds and the same visible wrong tries.
- They can send you an invite or a device link. Opening a device link asks you to choose Link before anything is sent, and an invite shows the inviter's name and fingerprint before you ask to be friends.
- They can put mail at a Lighthouse under tags of their own, up to its limits. Nobody fetches it.

### Friends

Someone you paired with, who holds your pair secret.

They can learn your name, personal message and status, when you're online, your device list with each device's name, each device's sealing key and your Lighthouses, and your IP address unless you always go through the relay. They get everything you write to them, each entry signed by your device, so they can show anyone that you wrote it: Pigeon's messages aren't deniable.

They can keep and copy what you sent them, choose what they're shown as (their name, presence, and the Lamport time of their own entries, where one far ahead is held and shown but your clock doesn't follow it), buzz you, remove you, which your Pigeon honours, and leave you mail.

They can't write as you or change an entry of yours, speak for your identity, find your other friends' rooms or your self room, change your name (a `profile` counts only from your own devices), or give you a sealing key for a device that isn't theirs (a key counts only when signed by the device it names, and only for a device on its owner's list).

Once you remove or block someone, your Pigeon forgets them, leaves your pair rooms and takes nothing more from them. A blocked person's request through your code is never shown, and an invite of theirs you open is refused before anything is agreed. They keep what you sent them before.

### Code holders

Someone who saw one of your codes or links: a live invite code (hosted for 30 minutes), a device-link code (10 minutes), the `#i/` or `#l/` link or QR code standing for either, or an invite link through Lighthouses (`#b/`, up to 7 days and 25 people). Each is a secret only for as long as it's open, and only as well as it was kept.

- **An invite code.** They can open it before your friend does. Their request shows in Add a Friend with their name, fingerprint and bird, and nothing happens until you choose Add. They can instead wait in the room for your friend and answer as the code's maker: your friend sees their fingerprint, not yours, and is told to compare it with your screen.
- **An invite's `#i/` link.** The same, except that your friend's Pigeon checks the offer's fingerprint against the link's (the first 8 bytes of the SHA-256 of your identity) and refuses anyone else's.
- **A device-link code or `#l/` link.** They can ask to be linked. Link a Device shows the asking device's fingerprint, which the handshake covered and which the device in your hand shows too; you choose Link only when they match. A device you link gets everything a linked device does (see [Stolen devices](#stolen-devices)). They can instead answer your new device as if they were your Pigeon: from a link, the new device refuses an identity whose fingerprint isn't the link's; from a typed code, it shows whose Pigeon answered for you to compare, and asks only after Continue.
- **An invite link through Lighthouses.** They can open its card, which holds your name, identity, device log, your devices' sealing keys and your Lighthouses, and ask to be friends, which you see and must add. They can't read anyone else's request, which is sealed to your devices, or its answer, which waits under that asker's own reply secret. A card someone else puts under the link's tag is refused unless its identity has the link's fingerprint (16 bytes) and it is signed by the device the link names (16 bytes of its hash), active on that identity's device log. They can put as many blobs under the tag as a Lighthouse lets them, but the device that made the link puts its cards claimed, which nobody without the link's claim key can add to, and whoever opens the link reads the claimed cards first, so junk only costs the opener the bounded read of everything else. A link made before cards were claimed is claimed once the device that made it next reaches each Lighthouse; if someone holding it claims its tag at a Lighthouse first, the card there stays unclaimed and can be buried as before, until the link is made again. A Lighthouse from before claims keeps nothing claimed, so a link whose card is only at one of those can still be buried.

### Stolen devices

A device of yours that someone else holds while it's still on your list: one left unlocked, or a copy of its browser profile. A copied profile holds the same device key; when both copies are open in the same room, each hears the other's `hello` with a different `instance` and says the Pigeon is open somewhere else.

- Until it's removed, it is you. Device keys are non-extractable, so nobody can copy them out of the browser, but the page there can use them. It reads your chats, writes as that device, and holds what any linked device holds: your friends and their pair secrets, the self secret, the blocked list and your invite links.
- If it's the device that writes your list (the first device, unless a recovery named another), it can add and remove devices, and only your recovery key takes the list back. If it isn't, it can't change the list at all.
- It can't replace a recovery key you've committed: where two copies of a log name different keys, the one a Pigeon had first stays. If you haven't committed one, a stolen first device can.

### Removed devices

A device taken off your list, by the device that writes it or by a recovery.

- Friends and your other devices that have the newer log take nothing more from it: its `hello` doesn't count, so nothing else it sends in a pair or self room does; its mail is left; and no mail is sealed to it.
- What it signed before it was removed still counts, by its own time, so chats catch up with no gaps. That time is the device's own word, so it can date an entry before its removal; see [Accepted residuals](#accepted-residuals).
- Anyone who hasn't seen the newer log yet still takes it as yours. The log goes to every pair room and the self room, in self mail and on invite cards, and the removed device is sent it too, so it stops talking.
- A removal is for good within its epoch: its key can't be added back, and entries it writes in an epoch a recovery has ended change nothing.
- It keeps what it already had: your chats so far, your friends, and their pair secrets and the self secret as they were. Removing it stops anything new from counting as you; it doesn't take back what it holds. But those secrets are replaced: the device that removed it begins a new round for each at once, sealed to the devices still on the lists ([Replacing a secret](protocol.md#replacing-a-secret)), so it can't find the new rooms or mail tags. Until a friend's Pigeon has taken the new pair secret (one that doesn't replace secrets after a removal takes it once it updates), your devices meet that friend in the old rooms too, where it can still see you both online, and mail to that friend goes under the old tags, sealed only to devices still on the lists.
- That includes the secret of every invite link it heard of, so it can still put cards under those links, signed on the list from before, and answer a request it can read. Removing it closes no link; what limits it is that a link names the device that made it and when it ends:
  - Through a link another of your devices made, it can do nothing. Whoever opens the link takes only the card of the device the link names, and only that device's answers, `added` or any other, so the removed device's cards and answers are ignored, before its removal as after: it can't add anyone, or tell anyone they were declined or the link closed, even with a log it signed on an epoch a recovery has since ended. Those links go on working.
  - Through a link it made itself, which it may never have told your other devices of, it is the device the link names, so whoever opens or asks through it before the link's end can be added by it, as you, and takes it as you until they hear your newer list some other way, as anyone who hasn't seen it does. That lasts until the link's end, at most 7 days after it was made, plus the two days an asker who asked before the end goes on taking answers. It can't stretch a link: the end is in the link, an open card that outlasts it is refused, nobody asks through a link on or after its end, and a link whose end is more than 7 days and a day off is refused. A link it made while still on the list to end further off opens only in its last week and a day.
  - A link from before links named their maker and end (`#a/`) no longer opens, so none of those is left to it.
  - An honest removed device stops talking, so its own links go silent rather than show as closed: whoever opens one sees its card and gets no answer.

### Recovery-code holders

Someone who has your current recovery code, on paper or in a file.

- It outranks every device: with it they can start your next epoch, choose the device that writes your list, keep or drop any device, and choose the next recovery key. A friend's Pigeon takes the recovery when it follows on from the log it keeps for you.
- Using the code replaces it with the next key it shows. The old code still counts until a friend has seen the new epoch, and two recovers for your latest epoch from the same key settle on the lower signature ([flows.md](flows.md#recovery-with-every-device-lost)).
- Without a committed recovery key, nobody can bring your identity back once every device is lost.

### Sealed-file holders

Someone who gets a copy of a file sealed with a passphrase: an export from Export My Data, or a recovery key saved as a file. They can try passphrases on their own hardware for as long as they like; nothing in the file limits the number of tries.

- **If a guess is right they get what the file holds.** An export holds your friends with their pair secrets, your chats, your friends' device lists, your profile and settings, but no private key and not your own device log or self secret ([protocol.md](protocol.md#your-data-in-a-file)); the pair secrets let them find your friends online, as [friends](#friends) can, though not read or write messages. A recovery file holds the recovery code, which makes them a [recovery-code holder](#recovery-code-holders).
- **What stops them is the cost of each guess.** The passphrase is stretched with Argon2id at the memory, passes and lanes written in the file. Files made now ask for 64 MiB and 3 passes over it, which takes about a second on a phone and under half a second on a desktop. A guess can't skip that memory, so a machine tries only as many guesses at once as it has memory for: about a thousand at once for every 64 GiB. Reading a file never costs more than 128 MiB and 6 passes, whatever its header says.
- **A short or common passphrase is still guessable.** Pigeon asks for at least 8 characters and doesn't check how guessable they are. Argon2id makes each guess dearer; it doesn't make a passphrase someone could guess in a few thousand tries safe. A long passphrase of several unrelated words is.
- **Files from before are weaker.** An export or recovery file from before this change is stretched with PBKDF2-SHA256 at 600,000 rounds, which needs almost no memory, so a GPU tries far more guesses at once. It opens as before, and copies of it stay as weak as they were. Exporting again makes a data file with Argon2id. A recovery file can't be saved again, since the code is shown once and never kept: its Argon2id replacement comes from using the key (Use My Recovery Key), which moves to a new epoch with a new code to save, and the old file's code goes on counting until a friend has seen that epoch.
- **The file and its passphrase are the whole defence.** Keeping them apart, as the dialogs say, is what stops a stolen copy from being opened at all.

## Out of scope

- **Whoever serves Pigeon's code.** Pigeon is a web page. Whoever controls the site, its hosting or its build can serve a page that reads everything. The Content Security Policy limits what the page may load, not what it's served.
- **The browser and the computer it runs on.** Extensions, malware, or someone at the unlocked browser have what the page has; the last is a [stolen device](#stolen-devices).
- **Watching the whole network.** Timing and sizes can still say who talks to whom, to someone who sees enough of the internet.
- **Denial of service.** Relays and Lighthouses can withhold, and anyone can fill a room or a Lighthouse up to its limits. What an attacker with many networks can do to those limits is under [Accepted residuals](#accepted-residuals).
- **Forward secrecy for mail.** Letters are sealed to each device's long-term sealing key, with no one-time prekeys, so whoever holds a device can open mail sealed to it that someone kept. A data channel's keys are fresh each connection, but chats are kept on both ends.
- **Deniability.** Every entry is signed by its device, as the [friends](#friends) section says.
- **Matching a fingerprint by brute force.** A live code's fingerprint is 8 bytes, so making an identity with someone else's takes about 2^64 tries; an invite link through Lighthouses carries 16 bytes.
- **What isn't built.** Files, pages, guestbooks, the lobby and letters carried by friends are specified in [protocol.md](protocol.md) but not built, so nothing here covers them.

### Accepted residuals

These are risks Pigeon knows about and doesn't build anything against, because it is a messenger for friends and small groups and each costs more to close than it protects. None of them reveals a message, a key or a secret, or lets anyone speak as you or a friend.

1. **Many networks.** An attacker with many networks, such as tens of IPv6 /48 ranges or hundreds of IPv4 addresses, can fill the budgets a Lighthouse keeps (its connections, its waited tags, its stored blobs) or make Lighthouses and clients do more work. The limits on one address or range stay, and they hold one network to its share; nothing holds the total against many. Whoever is shut out gets a refusal, and Pigeon falls back to looking for mail every 10 minutes, so mail still arrives.
2. **What your network and tag holders can infer.** Someone behind the same address as you, or anyone who knows a tag, can learn from where limits bite and from wakes: roughly how many tags you wait on, when your Pigeon is open, and, by aiming a tag prefix, a false wake. Pigeon pads nothing to hide it.
3. **A device of yours playing games while it is on your list.** The same goes for the window before your other devices hear it was removed. It can stamp an entry far ahead or at the cap, fork or leave gaps in its entry numbers, or backdate an entry. Your devices can then disagree, or its entries can count for longer. It can't reveal your messages or write as someone else. Removing the device and pairing again repairs it.
4. **A hostile Lighthouse making a client fetch a lot.** A Lighthouse on your list can list as much as it likes under your tags, and your client fetches it. Use Lighthouses you trust, and take one off your list if it misbehaves.

## Messages and their checks

Every protocol message, who signs it, what its signed bytes bind it to, and what the receiver checks before acting on it. A new message gets a row in the table for where it travels. The last column names the attackers above that the checks keep out.

### Envelopes

Every message in a room is an envelope `{ v: 1, from, room, at, body }`, signed by the device `from` over all of it. Before any row below, the receiver refuses one over 64 KB, malformed, with a body of the wrong shape for its `type`, naming another room, or whose signature isn't `from`'s in its one accepted spelling ([protocol.md](protocol.md#identity)). The room it names is the chat's id in pair rooms (the same in each daily room), `self:<identity>` in the self room, and the room's own name in invite and link rooms; `body.type` is in the signed bytes too, so an envelope can't count in another room or as another message.

Binding to a channel: in pair and self rooms a connection counts only once a bound `hello` proves which device it is, and then only for what that device sends on it. In invite and link rooms, everything after `pake-start` counts only on the same connection from the same device, and the sealed answers name the run's session, the SHA-256 of its transcript.

### Mail

Mail is a letter `{ v: "pigeon/letter/v1", room: "mail:<kind>", sender, to, date, id, head, from, at, body }`, signed by the device `from` of the identity `sender`, and sealed to each recipient device. It's sealed with HPKE (RFC 9180) when every recipient device says it opens it, with a commitment that names the kind, and otherwise in the older form every Pigeon opens ([protocol.md](protocol.md#lighthouses-and-mail)). Before any row below, the receiver opens it with its own sealing key, checks the blob's commitment so every device opens the same letter, checks the signature, refuses a letter whose kind isn't the one the HPKE form's commitment names, and refuses it unless `to` is its own identity, its kind is one the mailbox it fetched holds, and its date is within a day of the tag's. Each (sender, id) is taken once.

### Device logs

| Item                            | Signed by                          | Signed bytes bind                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Receiver checks                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Checked against                                        |

| Entry (`add`, `remove`, `name`) | The epoch's writer                 | `{ identity, epoch, seq, prev, kind, device, name?, at }`, with no `epoch` in epoch 0; the same with `v: "pigeon/device-entry/v1"` and `epoch` always is taken too. `prev` is the signature before it, compared as bytes. | Numbered and linked in one chain, signed by that epoch's writer, and allowed step by step: no device added twice or after its removal, the writer never removed.                                                                                  | Stolen devices, Removed devices                        |
| Recovery key                    | The first device                   | `{ identity, type: "recovery-key", key, at }`                                                                                                                                                                             | The identity's own signature; where two copies name different keys, the one held first stays.                                                                                                                                                     | Stolen devices                                         |
